Right Window · How a contract actually gets signed

The deadline is 21 February.
Your window shuts 29 November.

Every facade contractor in New York knows the Local Law 11 date. Almost none of them work backwards from it. This is what the ninety days before that date look like, day by day, for one contractor working one borough — with the real buildings, the real triggers and the real arithmetic from the build dated 31 August 2026.

90 days to sell it

A facade report is not a form. Engagement, then rope access or a scaffold drop, then the report itself, then the DOB NOW filing — call it six weeks of inspection and six weeks of paperwork, and both are conservative. An owner who has not signed by roughly 29 November cannot file by 21 February. That is the window, and it is the product: not who needs the work, but who has to decide about it this quarter.

Who is holding the phone

A facade engineering firm in Brooklyn. Two licensed inspectors, an average report at $12,000, and a close rate they put at three per cent because that is what their own cold calling has returned. They cover four ZIP codes: 11215, 11217, 11231. They have never bought a lead list and do not intend to.

The ninety days

Day 0Mon 31 Aug

Ten minutes, no account

They open the site. It asks one question — what do you do — and they pick Facade engineering. Two optional numbers follow: average contract and close rate. Both stay on the device.

The header rewrites itself. 800 buildings citywide, 200 in Brooklyn. Under the headline, one line of arithmetic they can check: $13.5M open → ~$404K expected by 21 Feb 2027, footnoted 800 shown + 322 more by then at this week's rate. Not a promise — the register's own arrival rate, measured over the last seven days, carried across the twenty-five weeks that remain.

Day 1, morningTue 1 Sep173 days left

Cut 800 down to a day's work

They type their three ZIPs into the search box — 11215 11217 11231 — and the whole page answers. The list becomes their territory. The pipeline figure moves with it, which is the moment most people stop treating it as marketing.

Then the filter chips, each carrying a live count. In Brooklyn: 61 buildings are paying for a sidewalk shed with no repair filed, 84 owe money at OATH, 18 have a hearing inside thirty days, 29 have a shed permit expiring inside sixty. These overlap. The intersection is the morning's call list.

Day 1, 10:40The first call

Open on their money, not on your service

The card is expanded. It names the managing agent, the firm's own phone number and where that number was read from. It names the penalty already assessed and the hearing already scheduled. Nothing here is a guess; every line links back to the city record it came from.

“I'm looking at 330 West 56th. You've had a sidewalk shed up for eight and a half years and there's no repair filed against it. DOB has $6,000 on the Cycle 9 report, and there's $41,250 open at OATH with a hearing on the 10th. The 10A filing is due 21 February and you haven't started. I can have a QEWI on the wall in three weeks.”

That is four public facts and one offer. The contractor did not research any of them; the card assembled them from five separate city datasets before the phone was picked up.

Day 1, afternoonThe second list

Sort by who answers, not by who is worst

Half the Brooklyn cards carry a phone number that resolves immediately — 105 of 200. Sorting by contact first puts those at the top, because a perfect lead you cannot reach is worth less than a good one you can. The rest carry a company and a role and a one-click web search.

Every call gets marked: Contacted, Won, Lost, or dismissed with a reason. Marking one Contacted also claims it in a shared pool, so nobody else on the product is working the same building.

Day 2Wed 2 Sep172 days left

The eighteen with a hearing this month

A different conversation, and a much shorter one. An owner walking into OATH in eight days with an open facade violation wants to arrive holding a signed engagement letter — it is the single most useful thing they can show. Citywide there are 59 of these inside thirty days; in Brooklyn, 18.

“Your hearing on 1199 East 53rd is Wednesday. You're going in with $33,750 open and nothing filed. If you engage today I can put the inspection on the calendar and you walk in with a signed scope instead of a promise.”
Days 3–5Thu–Sat

Call the landlord, not the building

The card names how many buildings that managing agent runs across the whole product. It is rarely one. AKAM Associates holds 38, Wavecrest 37, New Bedford 34, FirstService Residential 33. Within a single borough the clusters are still large — Douglas Elliman runs 13 in Queens, Wavecrest 10 in Brooklyn.

That changes what is being sold. Not one report at $12,000 but a portfolio at a rate, to a firm with one compliance officer and one deadline for all of them. One call, thirteen addresses, and the buyer has the same problem thirteen times.

Week 28–12 Sep~166 days left

Proposals, and a list that refilled itself

Twenty-odd conversations produce three or four proposals. Meanwhile the register moved: 15 new buildings and 97 new signals this week — a shed that just went up, a violation just written, an ownership change just recorded. The freshness strip counts only what the city itself published inside the window, so the number is small and true rather than large and meaningless.

The cards they dismissed taught the list something. Ask twice for the same reason — wrong borough, already has a contractor, nobody to call — and it stops offering that kind. Every rule it learned is listed with a one-click undo.

Weeks 3–615 Sep – 10 Oct

Close, and the reason the close is easy

The objection in this trade is never price; it is timing. “We'll look at it after the new year.” The answer is arithmetic, and it is on the card:

“After the new year is seven weeks before the filing date. You need six for the inspection and six for the report. If we start in January you file late, and late is $1,000 a month per building until you're not.”

Across the 800 facade cards the city has already assessed $57.6M in facade penalties, and $6.0M sits unpaid at OATH. The meter is not hypothetical; for most of these buildings it is already running.

29 NovThe last useful day90 days out

After this, you are selling next cycle

Anyone who has not signed by roughly here cannot file on time. The conversation changes from “let's get you compliant” to “let's minimise what you pay while you're not” — still a sale, a different one, and worth less. This is the date the product exists to put in front of somebody in August.

The rhythm afterwards

One email each morning with what the city published overnight for their trade and their borough, and nothing at all on a quiet day. That is the whole retention mechanism: the register keeps producing, and the contractor stops maintaining a list.

That is the unglamorous version and it is the one worth quoting. The interesting number is not the week; it is that the same list is still producing in November, and that the contractor spent no time building it. Over the twenty-five weeks to the deadline the arithmetic the site shows them is $404K expected — 800 buildings now plus 322 arriving at the rate the city has actually been publishing.

What this does not do

  • It does not dial, email or text anyone. The calls are the contractor's, and so are the TCPA and Do-Not-Call obligations that come with them.
  • It does not know who is already in conversation with a competitor. It knows who has filed nothing, which is not the same thing.
  • Roughly 42% of building cards carry a number that resolves immediately. The rest name a firm and a role, and a web search is one click away — but somebody still has to do it.
  • The carbon register has no deadline to sell against, and the gas register is legally uniform: one sub-cycle, one date, one issue sweep. Both are honest and neither is as strong as facades.
  • Nobody has paid for any of this yet. Every figure above is about the offer, not about demand.
